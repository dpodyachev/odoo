When does the SSL certificate expire? Tools for Linux and Windows – and what eMudhra has to do with it

TLS in one minute, online checks, openssl/dig/curl on Linux, PowerShell on Windows, what the verify codes mean, lessons from history and the current eMudhra case.

TLS in one minute

TLS (formerly SSL) does two things: it encrypts the connection between browser and server, and it proves that the server really belongs to that domain. The proof is the certificate: domain + public key + the signature of a certificate authority (CA).

What gets verified is a chain: server certificate → intermediate → root. Only the roots are stored in browsers and operating systems. If the intermediate is missing, or a browser stops trusting the root, even a "valid" certificate is suddenly worthless. And every certificate has a fixed expiry date (notAfter).

Checking online

  • SSL Labs Server Test – chain, expiry, protocols, grade A–F. Thorough, takes 1–2 minutes.
  • crt.sh – every certificate ever issued for your domain, from the Certificate Transparency logs. Ideal for finding forgotten subdomains and unexpected issuers.
  • badssl.com – test sites with deliberately broken certificates to practise on.

Linux: name and DNS first

Half of all "certificate problems" are DNS problems: the domain points to the wrong server, or a forgotten IPv6 record leads somewhere else.

Linux: openssl s_client

-servername matters: it sends SNI. Without SNI, a server hosting several domains often returns a completely different certificate – and you check the wrong one.

Verify return codeMeaning
0all good
10certificate has expired
18self-signed
19self-signed certificate in the chain (unknown root)
20issuer not found – intermediate missing or root not trusted
21incomplete chain (server does not send the intermediate)
62hostname mismatch (with -verify_hostname)

Windows: PowerShell

No extra software needed: DNS, port and certificate straight from PowerShell. If Git for Windows is installed, Git Bash also has openssl, so the Linux commands above work too.

What has already happened to certificates

  • 2018 – Symantec: after repeated failures, Chrome and Firefox distrusted all Symantec certificates. Millions of sites had to switch.
  • 30 Sep 2021 – DST Root CA X3: the old Let's Encrypt root expired. Modern browsers didn't notice, but old Android devices, OpenSSL 1.0.x and many scripts and integrations broke.
  • 2024 – Entrust: Chrome and others stopped trusting new Entrust certificates.
  • Shorter lifetimes: under CA/Browser Forum ballot SC-081 the maximum lifetime drops step by step: 200 days from March 2026, 100 days from March 2027, 47 days from March 2029. Let's Encrypt stopped sending expiry e-mails in 2025.

Right now: eMudhra (October–November 2026)

On 17 October 2026 it became known that eight intermediate certificates of the Indian CA eMudhra had been generated with weak, recoverable keys (key ceremony in April 2025). Mozilla distrusted the root emSign ECC Root CA – G3 on 20 October, Chrome on 27 October. On 22 October eMudhra announced its exit from the public TLS business. According to eMudhra and industry reports, the RSA root emSign Root CA – G1 follows: Microsoft removes it on 16 November, Apple on 30 November.

  • If eMudhra/emSign appears in the chain: reissue the certificate with another CA – RSA certificates from eMudhra are no long-term fix either.
  • Check CAA records: if the domain only allows emsign.com, no other CA can issue.
  • Not just web servers: load balancers, VPN, mail servers and appliances with manually imported certificates too.

General tips

  • Check from outside what is actually served – not the file on disk.
  • Always use SNI (-servername) and always check the whole chain.
  • Alert at 21, 14 and 7 days – not on the expiry day.
  • Keep an inventory of all certificates: domains, subdomains, mail, VPN, appliances; crt.sh helps to find them.
  • Automate renewal (see the certbot article) – with 47-day certificates there is no other way.
