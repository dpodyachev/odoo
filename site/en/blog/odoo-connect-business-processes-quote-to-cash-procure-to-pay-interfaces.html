How Odoo connects business processes: quote-to-cash, procure-to-pay and the interfaces that remain

What links sales, warehouse, purchasing and accounting inside one Odoo database, which settings decide when an invoice or vendor bill is created, where double entry disappears and where interfaces remain (shop, bank, DATEV, EDI, carriers) – and how to build those interfaces so they do not silently break.

The main promise of an ERP is simple: a piece of information is entered once and every department works with it. In Odoo this works well – as long as the process stays inside one database. Where it leaves the database (a web shop, a bank, the tax advisor, a carrier), you need interfaces, and that is where most of the effort and most of the problems are. This article follows two chains through Odoo and shows what connects them.

Quote-to-cash: from lead to reconciled payment

  1. Lead / opportunity in CRM – the contact and the expected deal.
  2. Quotation – created from the opportunity, so the link is kept; customer, prices and terms come from the master data.
  3. Sales order – confirming the quotation creates the delivery (or a manufacturing order) through the routes of the product.
  4. Delivery – the warehouse picks and ships; the delivered quantity is written back to the order lines.
  5. Customer invoice – created from the order, for ordered or delivered quantities (see below).
  6. Payment – registered manually or matched from the bank statement.
  7. Reconciliation – the payment closes the open item; the invoice shows Paid, the order Fully Invoiced.

Procure-to-pay: from reorder rule to paid vendor bill

  1. Reorder rule (min/max) or a sales order with the Buy route triggers a need.
  2. Request for quotation – created automatically by the scheduler for the preferred vendor of the product.
  3. Purchase order – after confirmation the expected receipt appears in the warehouse.
  4. Receipt – received quantities are written back to the purchase order lines.
  5. Vendor bill – created from the purchase order, so prices and quantities are compared with what was ordered and received.
  6. Payment – often as a batch payment or SEPA file, then reconciled with the bank statement.

What actually links the documents

  • Source document (origin) – every delivery, receipt and manufacturing order remembers which document created it. The search field Source Document finds the whole chain.
  • Procurement group – keeps all moves of one sales order together, also over several steps (pick → pack → ship) and across warehouses.
  • Invoicing policy on the product: Ordered quantities (invoice right after confirmation – services, prepayment) or Delivered quantities (invoice only what has left the warehouse). This one setting decides whether the invoice can run ahead of the goods.
  • Bill control on the purchase side: Ordered quantities or Received quantities. With received quantities, a vendor bill line cannot simply be paid for goods that never arrived. Enterprise adds a 3-way match status (should be paid / on hold) on vendor bills.
  • Analytic accounts and tags – carried from order to invoice, so the margin per project or department appears without a spreadsheet.

Where double entry disappears – and where it stays

Inside the database nobody types a customer address, a price or a quantity twice: the order feeds the delivery, the delivery feeds the invoice, the invoice feeds accounting. What remains manual are decisions – approving a discount, accepting a partial delivery, choosing which bills to pay – and everything that comes from outside. Building interfaces that do not silently break

An interface that works in the demo and loses one order a week in production is worse than none – nobody notices until a customer calls. Five rules we use for every integration:

  • Idempotency. Every incoming record carries the ID from the other system (an external ID or a dedicated field with a unique constraint). If the same order arrives twice, it is updated, not duplicated.
  • A queue instead of a direct call. Incoming data is stored first and processed by a job; with the OCA module queue_job failed jobs stay visible, can be retried and do not block the user who triggered them.
  • Retries with a limit. Network errors and locked records (could not serialize access) are normal – retry a few times with a pause, then mark the job as failed instead of looping.
  • A log per message. What came in, what was created, what failed and why – searchable in Odoo, not only in a server log file.
  • Monitoring. Failed jobs, a queue that stops moving, a bank import that has not run since yesterday – these must produce a message, e.g. to a Telegram bot, not wait for a user to notice.

Automation rules or code?

Odoo's automation rules (and server actions) are fine for small reactions inside the database: set a field, create an activity, send a template e-mail. As soon as an external system, a loop over many records or error handling is involved, a small module is the better choice: it is versioned in git, tested on a copy of the database and survives the next upgrade in a predictable way. A rule written in the interface is easy to create and easy to forget at the next migration.

Step by step: connect your processes

  1. Draw both chains for your company on one page – which documents, who approves, where data comes from outside.
  2. Set the policies first: invoicing policy per product category, bill control, routes. They decide how the documents follow each other.
  3. List every interface with direction, volume per day and the person responsible.
  4. Build interfaces with ID, queue, retry, log and monitoring – on a copy of the database first.
  5. Test the whole chain end to end with real cases: partial delivery, return, credit note, partial payment.
  6. Watch the first weeks: delivered-but-not-invoiced and received-but-not-billed lists show where the chain is broken.

Conclusion

Inside one Odoo database, sales, warehouse, purchasing and accounting are connected by design: a handful of settings decide the order of the documents, and double entry largely disappears. The real work is at the edges – shop, bank, DATEV, carriers, EDI. Interfaces built with idempotency, a queue, retries, logs and monitoring keep the chain reliable.
